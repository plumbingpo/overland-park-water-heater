Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Prairie Village

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush once a year if their water is hard.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The rating plate on the tank usually encodes the manufacture date, and its age tells you a lot. Most tank heaters make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units about 20. If your heater is past that point and is also showing discolored water, popping sounds, or moisture at the base, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than a rushed emergency install after a leak.

The sooner you call about a hot water problem, the smaller the repair tends to be. A sediment layer that's just starting to rumble can often be fixed with a flush, yet the same sediment, neglected through a few more years, can overheat the bottom of the tank and shorten the life of the tank, forcing a replacement much sooner. Save our number in your contacts now so help is one call away the moment something seems off. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Hot Water Headaches Prairie Village Homeowners Face

Around Prairie Village, water heater trouble tends to trace back to a handful of sources. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be part of the picture: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. When the sacrificial anode rod wears out, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the steel begins corroding from within, and a slow leak at the base is usually the first visible sign. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Well water can bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a properly routed T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak could damage finished space, and seismic straps in areas that require them. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most are much cheaper when caught early.
